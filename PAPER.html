Predicting the Wrong Thing Correctly

Evaluating machine-learning emulators of chaotic systems by the dynamics they preserve, not the forecasts they make

A research software paper on the lorenz63 package

Abstract

Machine-learning "emulators" are increasingly used as cheap stand-ins for expensive physical simulations. The standard way to train and judge them is forecast error: how close is the predicted next state to the true next state? This paper reports a case where that measure is actively misleading.

We study a neural network trained to emulate the Lorenz-63 system, the canonical model of deterministic chaos. We show that a model with near-optimal one-step prediction error 0.112 has, in a meaningful sense, learned no dynamics at all: its trajectories collapse onto a fixed point, its attractor has half the correct geometric dimension, and the rate at which it loses volume is 22 times too large. Forecast error does not notice.

We then build the measurement infrastructure needed to see this. The package implements eight diagnostics that characterise an emulator by dynamical invariants — how fast nearby trajectories separate, how much volume it contracts, what shape its attractor has, and how often it switches between the system's two lobes. Two design decisions make the numbers interpretable: (1) every metric is computed identically for a second true trajectory and for a deliberately trivial model, giving each number a ceiling and a floor; and (2) every experiment is repeated with five independent random seeds, so a difference can be compared against the noise rather than assumed real. The second decision forced us to retract two of our own earlier claims.

The central finding is that the emulator's failure is concentrated in a tail of states. The typical state contracts only 1.16× too fast, while the worst 5% contract 6.4× too fast. A metric reporting only the average would have hidden this. We trace the cause to a structural weakness of the standard approach: an emulator that learns the one-step map x → f(x) has nothing in its training signal that constrains how it changes volume, because the volume change enters the prediction only at second order in the timestep — invisible at the resolutions used in practice.

Finally we test a repair. An alternative parameterisation that learns the underlying velocity field dx/dt = g(x) and integrates it also fails, and for an instructive reason. But because the field exposes the volume change as a cheap derivative of its own output, it can be penalised directly. With the known volume-change rate added to the loss, the emulator's contraction accuracy improves from 4.6× wrong to 0.8% wrong, at a cost of about 30% in forecast error. The lesson is not that one parameterisation is better, but that a quantity you cannot measure inside the model, you cannot control.

All code, data and results are in this repository, and everything runs on a laptop CPU.

1. Introduction

1.1 The problem in one paragraph

Some physical systems are chaotic: they obey exact, simple laws, yet two runs started from almost identical conditions drift apart exponentially. Weather is the standard example. This makes long-range prediction impossible in principle, not just difficult in practice. The practical response has been to accept short-range predictions and try to get the statistics right — the climate rather than the weather.

Machine-learning emulators have become a popular way to do this cheaply. A neural network is trained on examples of a simulation and then used in its place, often 1000× faster. The field's standard quality measure is forecast error: average squared distance between the emulator's predicted next state and the true one.

This paper is about a specific worry: that forecast error can be excellent while the emulator has learned something with qualitatively wrong behaviour. We construct a demonstration where this happens, build the tools to detect it, and investigate the cause.

1.2 Why this is not a hypothetical

Our strongest example is an emulator whose one-step error is 0.112 on a system whose states span roughly ±20. That is a good number by any normal standard. Yet the same emulator, when rolled forward, produces a trajectory that contracts to a point rather than exploring the attractor.

The intuition for how this happens: chaos means errors grow and trajectories diverge. A model can achieve a low average error not by predicting correctly but by predicting conservatively — outputting something close to the average of what could happen. In a chaotic system, the "conservative" answer is close to the average of the attractor, which is exactly the sort of prediction that shrinks the dynamics. Forecast error rewards this; nothing in the objective punishes it.

1.3 Thesis

An emulator of a chaotic system must be evaluated on the dynamical invariants it preserves, not on the forecasts it makes. These two can be almost perfectly decoupled, and when they are, only the invariants reveal it.

A secondary thesis follows from the results:

The invariants that matter most are the ones the training signal cannot see. Whether an emulator can be repaired depends on whether the architecture exposes those invariants to the loss.

1.4 Contributions

  1. A demonstration of complete decoupling between forecast accuracy and dynamical fidelity (§4.1), with the numbers to make it concrete.
  2. A measurement suite of eight diagnostics (§3.3), each given a ceiling and a floor by explicit control experiments (§3.5) — without which none of the numbers would be interpretable.
  3. A resolvability screen (§3.6) that separates real differences between configurations from seed-to-seed noise. Applying it forced us to retract two prior claims (§4.2).
  4. A localisation result: the failure is a tail phenomenon, not a uniform bias (§4.3).
  5. A mechanistic explanation — the volume-change rate is invisible to a one-step training signal at practical timesteps (§5.1) — and a working repair (§4.4, §5.2).
  6. A documented set of implementation errors found and fixed along the way (§6), several of which had previously produced entirely spurious results.

2. Background for non-specialists

This section assumes no physics. Each idea is introduced with the intuition first.

2.1 The Lorenz-63 system

In 1963 Edward Lorenz distilled atmospheric convection into three coupled equations:

dx/dt = σ (y − x)
dy/dt = x (ρ − z) − y
dz/dt = x y − β z

with the standard parameters σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8/3. The variables x, y, z are not positions in physical space; they are abstract amplitudes (roughly, temperature contrasts and circulation strength). We use them simply as a three-dimensional test system with the right mathematical character and a known exact answer.

The equations say: the rate of change of each variable is a simple function of the current state. There is no randomness anywhere. Yet the resulting motion is chaotic.

2.2 Attractors

Plot the trajectory over a long time and you do not get a filled blob or a wandering path. You get a specific, intricate shape that the motion traces forever: the attractor. Lorenz's is the famous two-lobed "butterfly".

Two points about this matter for everything that follows:

  • The shape is stable. Although individual trajectories are unpredictable, the region they occupy is perfectly reproducible. This is why climate statistics are meaningful even though weather forecasts are not.
  • The shape is complicated. It has a fractional ("fractal") dimension slightly above 2 — more than a surface, less than a solid.

2.3 Chaotic means "sensitive to initial conditions"

Start two runs a hair apart. The separation grows exponentially:

separation(t) ≈ separation(0) × e^(λ t)

The Lyapunov exponent λ is the growth rate. A positive λ is the definition of chaos. For Lorenz-63 the largest is λ₁ ≈ 0.9 per unit time, so an initial separation of 10⁻⁵ is amplified by a factor of about e^(0.9 × 50) ≈ 10¹⁹ over 50 time units. Since the attractor is only about 40 units across, the two trajectories are effectively unrelated after roughly one time unit. This is why pointwise forecast comparison is useless beyond a short horizon: the error saturates at the size of the attractor and then merely fluctuates.

2.4 The Lyapunov spectrum: three numbers, not one

A three-dimensional system has three Lyapunov exponents, forming the spectrum. Each describes how fast a small volume is stretched or squashed along one independent direction.

For Lorenz-63 the textbook values are roughly:

exponent value meaning
λ₁ +0.906 fastest stretching — the chaos
λ₂ 0 neutral: the direction along the flow
λ₃ −14.57 strong contraction

λ₂ ≈ 0 is not a coincidence; it holds for any smooth flow, because moving along a trajectory costs nothing. This makes it a useful correctness check.

2.5 Volume contraction — the idea this paper turns on

Imagine a small blob of initial conditions. As the system evolves, the blob is stretched in some directions and squashed in others. Its volume changes at a rate given by the divergence of the equations, written ∇·f.

For Lorenz-63 the divergence is a constant:

∇·f = −(σ + 1 + β) = −13.667   everywhere

The key consequence: the true system destroys volume at exactly the same rate at every single point in space, with no exceptions. Stretching is wildly state-dependent, but the net volume change is rigidly constant.

And the Lyapunov exponents must sum to that rate:

λ₁ + λ₂ + λ₃ = −13.667

Check against the table: 0.906 + 0 − 14.57 = −13.66, which checks out.

This gives us a rare gift: a known, exactly constant, local target. If we measure how much volume an emulator destroys at a particular state and compare it with −13.667, any discrepancy is purely the emulator's error — we never have to ask whether the true system would have behaved differently there.

We use this in §4.3 to locate exactly where the emulator goes wrong.

A note on signs. "Contraction rate" below means the rate of change of log-volume, d(ln V)/dt, which equals ∇·f. For Lorenz it is negative (volume shrinks). We sometimes report a ratio of the emulator's rate to the truth's. Both are negative, so a ratio above 1 means the emulator contracts faster than it should, and a ratio below 1 means it contracts too slowly.

2.6 Two ways to build an emulator

This distinction is central to §4.4 and §5.

The map approach. Learn a function that takes the state now and returns the state one step later:

x_(n+1) = f(x_n)

Training is simple and direct. But the volume change is an emergent property of the whole network — the determinant of f's derivative matrix. There is no single number you can point at and say "this is the volume change".

The field approach. Learn the velocity field instead, then integrate it with a standard numerical solver (here, classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta):

dx/dt = g(x)      →      x_(n+1) = RK4(g, x_n, Δt)

Now the volume change has a name: the divergence of g, ∇·g. It is computable from g's output by a simple derivative. That makes it something you can measure inside the model — and therefore something you can add to the loss.

3. What the software does

Everything is in the lorenz63 Python package. It runs on a CPU with NumPy alone; an optional JAX backend exists for speed.

3.1 Pipeline overview

The package is a straight line from generating data to reporting a number, with the controls branching off in parallel at the measurement stage:

Stage Module What it does
1. Ground truth system.py Integrates the Lorenz equations with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta stepper; also supplies the exact derivative matrices the diagnostics compare against.
2. Data data.py Cuts long trajectories into sliding windows (x_n, x_(n+1), …) and adds noise to the inputs only.
3. Model models.py, field_models.py The emulator itself: a map network or a velocity-field network.
4. Training train.py, jax_trainer.py Adam with gradient clipping; one loss definition shared by both backends.
5. Rollout controls.py Runs the trained emulator forward, and separately builds the two control trajectories.
6. Measurement diagnostics/ Eight pure functions. They return numbers; they never plot and never decide pass or fail.
7. Reporting report.py Builds the figures and results.json, and re-reads every image it writes to confirm it is not blank.
8. Aggregation aggregate.py Collapses the five seeds into means and spreads, and computes the pairwise resolvability verdicts.

Two structural rules are enforced by tests rather than convention, because both were violated in the original code:

  • Diagnostics never create figures. Plotting lives only in report.py. A test parses the diagnostic modules and fails if any of them imports a plotting library.
  • Measurement and judgement are separate. No diagnostic prints "PASS" or "FAIL"; deciding whether a difference is meaningful happens in aggregate.py, where the controls and seed spreads are visible.

3.2 The emulator

A small neural network ("multi-layer perceptron"): three inputs, three outputs, three hidden layers of 128 units each, with a smooth activation function. 33,923 parameters. Two variants share this architecture exactly, so comparisons are parameter-for-parameter fair:

  • models.py — NumpyMLP, the map emulator, plus an IdentityModel used as a deliberately useless control.
  • field_models.py — VectorFieldMLP, the field emulator. Its step() integrates one Runge–Kutta step of the learned field, so it is interchangeable with the map everywhere downstream.

The field emulator's gradients pass backwards through the integration. That is unusual enough that we check it against numerical finite differences rather than trusting it: agreement is 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ (tests/test_field_models.py).

3.3 The diagnostics

Each is a pure function (ground_truth, emulator, context) → (true_metrics, emulator_metrics). None of them plots anything, and none of them decides pass or fail — that separation is enforced by a test, because it was the root cause of the original pipeline producing 28 completely blank image files.

Diagnostic Plain-language question it answers
lyapunov_spectrum How fast does it stretch and squash, versus the true rates?
local_contraction Where does it get the volume change wrong, and does it stretch in the right places?
error_growth How fast do two nearby trajectories separate, by a route independent of the spectrum?
invariant_measure Does it spend its time in the same regions, with the same distribution?
correlation_dimension Does its attractor have the right geometric dimension?
symbolic_dynamics Does it switch between the two lobes as often as it should?
dmd_spectrum Does its oscillation content match?
vector_field_slice Does its local rule agree with the true one, away from where it was trained?

local_contraction returns both the volume-change error and the local stretching rate, so the eight modules yield nine reported quantities.

Note that error_growth estimates the largest Lyapunov exponent by a completely different route — following two nearby trajectories — making it an independent check on the spectrum calculation rather than a restatement of it. The two agree to within 8% on the well-behaved configurations.

The self-check design matters. The Lyapunov diagnostic reports two quantities that must hold if the implementation is correct:

  • sum(λ) == ln|det A| / Δt, exactly, where A is the one-step flow Jacobian; and
  • to leading order, that equals the constant divergence −13.667.

If either fails, the diagnostic is wrong and we know before drawing any conclusion. In our runs sum(λ) matches the reference to five decimal places at every configuration, and the ground-truth values (1.02, 0.10, −14.89) sit within a few percent of the textbook (0.906, 0, −14.57).

3.4 The dataset and what "noise" means here

We generate long ground-truth trajectories, cut them into many short sliding windows, and treat each window as one training example. Noise is added to the inputs only:

x_train = x_clean + ε,     targets stay clean

This models the realistic situation where you observe a system imperfectly but want to learn its true dynamics. The sweep varies ε over 0.05, 0.1, 0.4 and the forecast horizon over 10 and 20 steps, giving four configurations.

3.5 Controls — giving every number a scale

This is the design decision that makes the results mean anything. For every metric we also compute it for:

  • A second true trajectory, started from a different initial condition. This is the ceiling: how different do two genuinely correct trajectories look under this metric? If the emulator scores no worse than this, the metric has found nothing.
  • The identity model, x_(n+1) = x_n — a model with no dynamics whatsoever. This is the floor, and it caught a serious design flaw in an early version of our own test suite: a test that declared success when perturbed trajectories diverged was passing for the identity model too, because divergence is a property of chaos, not of fidelity.

3.6 Replicates and the resolvability screen

Each configuration is run with five independent random seeds, varying both the sampled training data and the network initialisation. For every metric we then report the mean and spread, and for every pair of configurations we ask:

Is the difference between these two means larger than the combined standard error?

We call this resolvable. It is a crude screen — roughly a one-sigma test — not a formal hypothesis test, and we say so in §7. But it is the difference between reporting a result and reporting a number.

4. Results

4.1 Forecast accuracy and dynamical fidelity are decoupled

The headline demonstration. At noise level 0.4, our emulator reaches a one-step error of 0.112 — competitive with the well-behaved configurations. Here is what else is true of it:

Quantity Ground truth Emulator Verdict
one-step forecast error — 0.112 looks fine
dominant Lyapunov exponent λ₁ +0.99 −0.006 chaos destroyed
volume contraction rate −13.67 −301 22× too fast
attractor dimension 2.06 0.85 collapsed
lobe switches per 1000 steps 30 ~1 stuck in one lobe
error-doubling time 0.70 4.3 6× wrong

The emulator has not learned a chaotic system. It has learned something closer to a fixed point. The forecast metric — the thing it was trained and would normally be judged on — reports none of this.

Figure 2 — symbolic_dynamics at noise 0.4. The emulator's lobe-switching (red, dashed) flatlines almost immediately while the true system (blue) continues to alternate; the title records a switch rate 0.04× the truth. The left panel shows the correspondingly impoverished return map.

4.2 Five seeds changed our conclusions

Running with replicates did not merely add error bars; it overturned two of our own findings. With a single trajectory per configuration we had reported that the dominant Lyapunov exponent was "off by a factor of 13" and that the lobe-switch rate was "3× wrong". Both were artefacts of picking one seed.

Claim Single run Five-seed mean Status
λ₁ is ~13× too small 0.08 0.68 ± 0.25 retracted — consistent with ~30% accuracy
lobe-switch rate 3× wrong 0.33 0.96 ± 0.38 retracted — dominated by seed noise
volume contraction too fast — 2.9 ± 0.3 holds, separates all 6 pairs

The volume contraction, and only the volume contraction, survived. We report this because it is the methodological point of the paper: the two retracted claims were more dramatic than the true result, and would have been easy to publish.

Ranking all metrics by how many configuration pairs they can distinguish:

metric pairs separated (of 6)
λ₃ (contraction) 6 / 6
sum(λ), divergence_error, contraction spread 5–6 / 6
λ₂, growth_rate 4–5 / 6
λ₁, lobe-switch rate, attractor energy 2–3 / 6
attractor dimension 2 / 6

Note the pattern: shape statistics are stable but insensitive, while contraction separates everything. The emulator gets the attractor's geometry roughly right and its rate of change of volume badly wrong.

4.3 The failure is concentrated in a tail of states

This is the most useful single result, and it required a purpose-built diagnostic.

Because Lorenz-63's divergence is exactly constant (§2.5), we can measure the emulator's volume change at each individual state and compare it against a known number. There is no ambiguity about what the answer should be.

The distribution is not a shifted version of the truth. It is a wide spread centred on the wrong value:

ground truth emulator
volume change, mean −13.67 (constant) −39.1
volume change, spread across states 9e-5 (numerical floor) 23.2
typical state (95th percentile) 1.00× 1.16× too fast
worst 5% of states 1.00× 6.4× too fast

Two things to take from this:

  1. The average hides the problem. A metric reporting only the mean (2.9×) would suggest a uniform bias, and would direct any repair at the average. In fact the bulk of states are nearly correct and a minority are catastrophically wrong. This single fact changes what a fix should target.
  2. The emulator invents structure the system does not have. The true volume change is rigidly constant: measured with the exact autodiff Jacobian the spread is 9e-5, which is round-off, not physics. The emulator's varies by 23 units across states. It has learned a state-dependent rule where none exists.

Correction. An earlier version of this measurement reported the true spread as 0.4 and attributed it to RK4 discretisation. That was wrong: it came from a hand-derived stage-product approximation to the flow Jacobian. Differentiating the RK4 step with autodiff instead gives the exact Jacobian, and the true spread drops by three orders of magnitude. The emulator's error was therefore about 60x larger relative to the floor than first stated, and the flow-Jacobian routine in the archived package is only accurate to ~1%.

Stratifying by position shows the error is organised, not random — worst in the mid-lobes, mildest near the saddle point between them:

region excess contraction
near the saddle (\|x\| < 2) −8
mid-lobes (6 ≤ \|x\| < 10) −40

Figure 1 — local_contraction. Left: the true volume-change rate is a single spike at −13.67; the emulator's is smeared across −14 to −110. Middle: the error grows with distance from the saddle point. Right: it falls with altitude. In the bottom-right panel the emulator's contraction bar (red) dwarfs the true one (blue), which is the failure that forecast error does not see.

We verified the emulator's derivative matrix against an exact analytical computation, agreeing to 5 × 10⁻⁹, so this is a real property of the trained network and not numerical noise.

4.4 Testing a repair: map versus field parameterisation

If the volume change is invisible to the training signal, can a different architecture expose it? We compared three arms, identical in data, architecture, parameter count, optimiser and budget:

arm what it is
map the standard approach: learn x → f(x)
field learn dx/dt = g(x), integrate with Runge–Kutta
field_div as field, plus a penalty on the field's divergence

The field parameterisation alone does not help. It fits the one-step increments better than the map, while learning a field whose divergence is −2 to −5 instead of −13.67. The reason turns out to be fundamental, and is the subject of §5.1.

A cleaner piece of evidence for the same conclusion: the unpenalised field's overall scale is itself unstable, coming out at 1.26 on one seed and 15.43 on another — a factor of twelve — while both fit the increments to the same accuracy. When the quantity that controls the volume change is unconstrained, the fit is free to place it anywhere.

With the penalty it works. Scanning the penalty weight (reduced dataset):

0 (none) 0.218 0.16× −2.19 3.74
0.01 0.250 0.53× −7.30 0.40
0.1 0.353 0.67× −9.15 0.60
1.0 0.610 0.99× −13.58 0.82
4.0 0.804 0.99× −13.68 0.43

At the full configuration, across all four settings:

configuration map field field_div
contraction accuracy, 0.05 noise 2.49× 1.39× 0.992×
0.1 noise 4.62× 4.73× 0.992×

The repair is near-exact and stable across every configuration, and it also removes the spurious state-dependence (spread collapses by a factor of ~1000). It costs roughly 30% in forecast accuracy — a price that can be dialled explicitly rather than paid unknowingly.

The asymmetry is the finding, and we return to it in §5.2.

5. Discussion

5.1 Why the training signal cannot see the volume change

This is the mechanistic explanation for everything above, and it is a dimensional argument.

Integrate the field over one timestep and expand in Δt:

x_(n+1) = x_n + Δt·g(x_n) + (Δt²/2)·(Dg·g)(x_n) + …

The divergence of g — the volume change — does not appear at all in the leading term. It first enters at second order in Δt. With Δt = 0.01 used throughout this work, that term is a factor of 100 smaller than the leading one.

So a training signal built from one-step increments is, to an excellent approximation, blind to the divergence. A field with five times the correct divergence still predicts the next state almost perfectly. Nothing in the objective penalises it, so nothing corrects it.

This has an important consequence beyond Lorenz-63: the problem is not the architecture, it is the information content of the objective. The field parameterisation does not make the volume change correct by construction — it merely relocates where the freedom lives. What it does is different and more useful.

5.2 Measurable inside the model ⇒ controllable

The map emulator's volume change is the determinant of its derivative matrix — an emergent property of the entire network. There is no local quantity to attach a penalty to.

For the field emulator, the volume change is ∇·g, a first derivative of the network's output with respect to its input. It is cheap, local, and differentiable. It can be measured, and therefore it can be put in the loss.

That is the transferable conclusion:

A quantity you cannot measure inside the model, you cannot control.

The architecture matters not because it imposes physics for free, but because it exposes physics to the optimiser.

5.3 What this implies for the wider field

Three practical consequences, in decreasing order of confidence:

  1. Do not report forecast error alone. Our noise-0.4 emulator has excellent forecast error and no dynamics. Reporting only error would have been a false claim of success.
  2. Check the invariants that are cheap and exactly known. Here the divergence is constant, so the target is exact. In general, any analytically known conservation law or structural property (symmetry, incompressibility, energy conservation) is the cheapest high-value check available.
  3. If an invariant is measurable inside the model, put it in the loss. The repair in §4.4 did not require more data, a larger network, or a better optimiser. It required telling the model about a quantity it was already capable of representing but had no reason to learn.

5.4 The limitation of the repair, stated plainly

This must be said clearly, because it bounds the result. The penalty in §4.4 uses the value −13.667, which we know because we computed it analytically for Lorenz-63. That is partly circular: we told the model the answer to the question we then measured.

The result is still meaningful, in two ways:

  • It establishes a mechanism and a bound: the architecture can represent correct volume dynamics, the failure was one of incentive, and the size of the achievable improvement is near-total.
  • It identifies the real open problem, which is estimating the invariant when it is not known — from data, from a partially known model, or from a surrogate. For a system whose divergence varies in space and time, this is genuinely hard and is the natural next experiment.

What the experiment does not show is a general-purpose method that improves chaotic emulators without external knowledge.

6. Implementation errors found, and why they matter

We record these because several produced entirely spurious scientific results, and because they illustrate how easily a measurement pipeline can report confident nonsense.

# Error Consequence
1 The loss function with the distributional and Lyapunov terms had no call sites — training used a different function Every reported number came from plain mean-squared error. The additional terms never trained anything.
2 The random seed was never split between sweep configurations All four "different noise levels" reused the same initial conditions and the same noise pattern. The sweep axis was largely fictitious.
3 The figure routine called show() before savefig() 28 of 32 committed images were fully transparent files containing nothing. They had been cited as evidence.
4 A "trajectory uniqueness" test passed when perturbed runs diverged It was testing for chaos, which the identity model also exhibits. It could not fail for any model.
5 A dwell-time metric divided by a count using a 1e-12 epsilon A trajectory with zero lobe crossings reported 6.2 × 10¹², which then dominated every average and every ranking.
6 The divergence penalty omitted its dependence on the field's scale factor The divergence is linear in that scale, so the penalty could not control it at all: it drove the learned divergence to −101 while targeting −13.67.
7 The comparison script passed an arm name where a backend was expected Two of three experimental arms silently trained the same model, producing identical results that looked like a null finding.

Errors 1–3 were present in the original codebase. Errors 4 and 5 were ours, found because diagnostics were tested against known answers and because controls existed to catch them. Errors 6 and 7 were ours, found because the divergence penalty's gradient was checked against finite differences (error 6) and because arms that should have differed produced identical numbers (error 7) — a discrepancy that only a designed comparison reveals.

7. Limitations and threats to validity

  • The resolvability screen is crude. It compares a difference against the sum of two standard errors — approximately a one-sigma test, with no stated false-positive rate. "Within noise" here means "not distinguishable by this study", not evidence of equivalence.
  • Five replicates give a rough spread estimate. The uncertainty on a standard deviation estimated from five samples is itself around 35%.
  • Single system. All results are for Lorenz-63: three dimensions, one attractor, constant divergence. Whether the conclusions transfer to higher-dimensional or spatially extended systems is untested. This is the most significant limitation.
  • The repair uses a known answer (§5.4).
  • The comparison arms use two replicates, fewer than the five-replicate main study, so their error bars are weaker — though the effects reported there are far larger than the spread.
  • Training was not tuned per arm. All arms share hyperparameters, which keeps the comparison fair but means no arm is at its individual best.

9. Conclusion

We set out to test whether a machine-learning emulator of a chaotic system can achieve good forecast accuracy while getting the system's dynamics qualitatively wrong. It can, and the example is stark: an emulator with near-optimal one-step error that has collapsed to a fixed point.

We built a measurement suite that detects this, anchored at both ends by control experiments so that each number has a scale. Repeating each configuration with five seeds forced us to retract two of our own claims — both the most dramatic ones — and left a single robust finding: the emulator destroys volume far too fast, and does so unevenly.

Localising that error required exploiting a special property of the system — a rigidly constant volume-change rate — which let us show that the failure is concentrated in a minority of states, not spread uniformly. This is not a distinction a summary statistic would have revealed, and it changes what a repair should target.

Chasing the cause produced the paper's central mechanism. The volume change is invisible to a one-step training signal because it enters only at second order in the timestep — a factor of 100 down at the resolutions used here. The standard map parameterisation offers no way to fix this, because its volume change is not a quantity that exists anywhere inside the model. An alternative parameterisation that learns the velocity field does expose it, and once penalised directly the contraction accuracy improves from 4.6× wrong to 0.8% wrong.

The general lesson is narrower and more useful than "use a better architecture":

Whether an emulator can be made faithful to a physical invariant depends on whether that invariant is measurable inside the model. If it is, it can be controlled. If it is not, no amount of training will constrain it.

The obvious next question is whether the invariant can be constrained without being handed over analytically — that is, estimated from data. That is where we would go next.

Appendix A. Glossary

Term Meaning
Attractor The shape a dissipative system settles onto; trajectories approach it and stay. Stable even when individual paths are unpredictable.
Chaos Exponential sensitivity to initial conditions. Formally, a positive Lyapunov exponent.
Divergence ∇·f; the rate at which a small volume of initial conditions changes. Negative means volume shrinks. Constant −13.667 for Lorenz-63.
Emulator A cheap learned surrogate for an expensive simulation.
Field / vector field The rule giving the instantaneous rate of change, dx/dt = g(x).
Fixed point A state where nothing changes. An emulator that collapses has effectively learned one.
Jacobian The matrix of all first derivatives of a function. Its determinant gives the local volume change.
Lyapunov exponent Exponential stretching (or contraction) rate of nearby trajectories. Positive = chaotic.
Map A rule giving the next state directly, x → f(x).
Resolvable A difference larger than the combined standard error, so unlikely to be seed noise.
Runge–Kutta (RK4) A standard, accurate numerical method for stepping a differential equation forward.
